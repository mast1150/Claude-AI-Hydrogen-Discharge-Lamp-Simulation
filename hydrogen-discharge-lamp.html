Hydrogen discharge lamp

Electrons cross a tube of hydrogen gas. A collision can lift an atom's electron to a higher energy level; when it drops back, the atom gives off a photon whose color matches the energy it lost.

What you are seeing

  • Collisions. An electron with too little energy passes by unchanged. With enough, it can lift the atom's electron to a higher level and loses exactly that energy (shown as −x eV).
  • Photons. An excited atom drops back after a short delay and emits one photon. Lavender photons are ultraviolet and dull red ones are infrared, which your eye can't see.
  • Timing. Real lifetimes are nanoseconds. They are stretched to about a second so you can watch, and the choice of lower level uses hydrogen's real transition rates.
  • Atom motion. Atoms drift at random. The position setting only shapes where they spend their time, so clusters form and dissolve instead of staying fixed.
  • Limits. Levels n = 1 to 6 are modeled. Ionization at 13.6 eV is not.
  • Circuit. Electrons leave the negative terminal, cross the gas to the anode and return to the battery. Conventional current is drawn the opposite way.
